## 1. Before We Go — intimacy and individual style

Proposed 30–35 seconds per film. Friends getting ready to leave a lived-in apartment; the interesting event is how people help one another without dressing alike. Clothes are the action, not a product insert pasted into an unrelated plot.

Male: begin with a teen rejecting an older friend's attempt to straighten his chosen silhouette, gently and playfully. The older friend concedes. Elsewhere someone checks a reflection in a dark window, another retrieves his keys, another waits seated. A friend spots a twisted sleeve and gets permission with an offered hand before fixing it. End in the stairwell: one waits on the landing for the last person; they continue together at different paces. No uniform march or camera-facing formation. Relationships are fictional casting direction, not asserted family biography.

Female: begin mid-preparation, not entrance. The teen sneakerhead is seated finishing her laces, charcoal denim resting naturally over the shoes. An adult offers one styling suggestion; she makes her own choice. Another woman uses the window reflection to check her jersey, while a friend retrieves a bag left on a chair. At the door, the woman ahead looks back, notices the teen still finishing, and sits beside her instead of hurrying her. The teen finishes; both rise and follow. The pause resolves through mutual ease, not an adult approving the teen's appearance.

Style: window light, eye-level proximity, held fronts, medium/full-body coverage and small observed gestures. No scripted laughter, coordinated arm-folds or demand that every performer smile. Cast personalities through rehearsal, not racial shorthand.

Risk: becoming a generic getting-ready montage. Keep two specific interactions, coherent space and a beginning/end; keep product-visible holds inside the action. Avoid reducing women to beauty preparation or men to dominance.

## 2. Our Own Image — authorship and reversal

Proposed 30–40 seconds per film. The group makes its own photographs. The camera operator initially controls what counts as a good image; the photographed people change that process.

Male: a teen is told where to stand through a visible hand gesture. He moves a chair to a place he prefers and sits his own way. Photographer follows his decision. Other subjects choose different distances, posture and eyelines; they react to each other between exposures. The teen then notices the photographer never appears, takes the camera and makes his portrait. End on the photographer becoming a person seen by his friends, not on five people posing together.

Female: an adult photographer sets a conventional pose with a gesturing hand, then lowers it as the teen sneakerhead demonstrates the angle she wants. They trade places. The teen directs the adult into her own chosen position; another woman holds the reflector and turns it when the subject changes direction. End with the adult seeing the back-of-camera result and returning a look of recognition to the teen. No legible invented camera-screen interface or generated photo proof; use a source-bound insert if needed, or frame only hands and shared eyeline.

Style: deliberate composed frames alternating with relaxed intervals. No imitation camera-flash effect needed; the films remain silent. Camera is an actual story object with explicit ownership and hand continuity.

Risk: closest to the rejected posing films. Reject any treatment whose ending is again a group portrait. Camera handoff and changed authorship must read in motion without explanation.

## 3. A Place for Us — shared making and belonging

Proposed 35–40 seconds per film. People build a small occasion together. The gathering becomes personal because each person changes the space.

Male: five friends arrange a courtyard table. One arrives with a chair different from the others. Instead of replacing it to make the arrangement match, they change the spacing to fit it. Small practical contributions reveal different characters. End seated at uneven angles, already engaged with one another; the distinctive chair is visibly part of the table. No forced symbolic speech, hardship story or sweeping reveal. Avoid letting furniture work conceal the jerseys for most of the film.

Female: five friends prepare a temporary display of their own portraits in a studio/courtyard. An adult reaches for a polished posed image; a teen offers a candid one showing a shared unguarded moment. They compare, choose to include the candid, and rearrange the wall to make space. End with the person in the photograph recognizing herself and turning to its maker. Printed imagery must be approved source-bound assets; do not fabricate historical/family photographs or likenesses.

Style: daylight becoming warmer, tactile everyday objects, small useful actions. Product fronts get dedicated pauses before/after carrying objects. Cast does not exist to serve a protagonist; each performer has an observable contribution.

Risk: logistical and prop complexity, artwork continuity, and a possible generic community-ad ending. Keep it small and specific; do not turn Black identity into a checklist of cultural props.

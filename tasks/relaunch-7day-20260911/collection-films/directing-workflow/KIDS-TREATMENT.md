## The story we are adapting

The current source is [Corey's September 13 interview](FOUNDER-INTERVIEW-2026-09-13.md), not a generic succession story. He wanted to give his daughter a father, something to be proud of, and future comfort and independence. He continued building despite sacrifice so she could receive the benefits. He did not ask her to repay him, take over the company, or perform gratitude. The private circumstances of single parenthood are outside this film.

The proposed film makes the relationship visible: **a father finishes preparing a place for her, remains present as she enters it, and lets her choose how to enjoy what he has made possible.** His work and her freedom occupy the same scene. This is a fictional adaptation of Corey's commitment, not a claim to recreate a documented event or to show him building a real train. The father's screen identity and wardrobe are explicit casting decisions still to be selected; an unbound actor is a production dependency, not a reason to remove the father from the written story.

Visual reasoning draws on Fear of God's family-centered restraint, Kith's garment-led framing, and Oaklandish's specificity of place. The train gives the child an observable desire and an environment made usable at her scale. It is not a royal arrival or a ride through product displays.

## The film — beginning, middle, end

**Beginning: he is still there, doing the work.** Inside the Town Line's observation carriage, a father finishes the last fastening beneath a long, low upholstered bench. He tests its fixed edge, puts the hand tool into a small case, and clears the seat. The shot is brief and precise, not a renovation montage. In the same frame, the red product-card child waits at the bay entrance in the actual Kids set. Purple is already present farther along the aisle. They look past the camera toward someone approaching. Cut to reveal illustrated Skyy in her exact red Kids outfit. The father looks up at her; she goes directly to him, placing her hand briefly in his open hand. That voluntary familiarity establishes their fictional relationship without calling either card child his child.

**Middle: his presence enables her choice.** He rises beside her and moves his tool case clear of the route into the bay. He does not pull her forward or demonstrate where she must sit. Skyy releases his hand and notices the daylight seam beside the lowered interior shade. Its recessed, cordless handle is within her easy reach. She takes it herself. He remains beside the entrance, watching attentively rather than disappearing once the work is done.

Skyy lifts the assisted shade. The action is mechanical and believable, unrelated to the train's operation. The window is fixed glazing. As the shade rises, the camera opens beyond the cramped work detail to the full breadth of the observation bay: a generous low bench and panoramic view, made usable at the children's scale. His final small task now belongs to something much larger than the opening showed. Her action completes the reveal; he does not present it with a theatrical flourish.

**End: she chooses how to use what has been made possible.** The fully raised shade exposes a single panoramic window spanning the whole observation bay, from below the children's seated sightline to above their heads. Beyond it is Oakland's industrial waterfront, informed by the brand's place canon but treated as a designed film location until geographically verified. The low black upholstered bench runs beneath the entire view; the stepped/fluted source-hero outline frames the bay. The camera had held us at one narrow opening and now reveals its full width.

Red and purple have gathered just behind Skyy, still looking through the first opening. She notices them there. Rather than occupying the center, she chooses the bench's open end and sits without climbing or needing to be lifted. That movement exposes the central approach: red moves into the middle, while purple takes the adjoining place. Three people who were clustered at one slit now independently occupy the breadth of the carriage. Nobody asks Skyy to give something up; she has chosen a comfortable place of her own.

Her father takes a place beside the end of the bench, close enough to remain part of her space, rather than supervising from a distant doorway. Skyy looks back at him once; he meets her glance without asking for thanks or directing another action. She returns to her view. The final composition holds both his quiet presence and the children comfortably wearing the clothes. The closed tool case stays low at the edge of frame: the work has stopped long enough for him to be with her. Cut there.

The opening shows his commitment through work and presence. The middle gives her control over a space that effort has prepared. The ending keeps him with her while she enjoys it on her own terms. The vista and the full Kids ensemble are revealed by her choice. There is no pose exchange, coronation, bow, gratitude tableau, tearful parent montage, or obligation to follow her father's path. No misery, private family event, or supposed historic sacrifice is invented to explain the premise.

## Creative review

The main unresolved creative risk is **a viewer may read a thoughtfully prepared train outing rather than understand the scale of a father's continuing commitment.** The father is now present in the work, the first contact, and the ending; the scene must make that relationship more memorable than the shade mechanism. Keep the preparation brief and the final shared presence substantial enough to read. It expresses commitment and independence, not the entire history of sacrifice. The uncast father is an explicit production dependency, not a missing plot beat or an approved new identity.

A silent review must establish whether viewers recover the causal chain (father completes the space → welcomes her → she acts independently → he stays with her as she enjoys it), remember Skyy and the actual products, and recognize a caring relationship without assuming that the actor is the real founder. Do not tell them the answer first. If viewers describe furniture assembly or a generic train reveal instead, return the failed layer to development; do not claim that a logo, luxury set, or explanatory caption fixes it. The shade-only fallback removes the direct father relationship and therefore needs a separate founder choice; it is not an equivalent low-cost substitute.
